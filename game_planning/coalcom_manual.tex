\section{Welcome to the Job}

It is 6:00 AM on a Monday morning.

You are sitting in the control room of the Riverside Coal Power Station, 
watching steam pressure gauges and listening to the hum of turbines.

Three months ago you were an IT consultant. You had a comfortable office job,
spent your days in meetings about "synergising cross-functional paradigms,"
and your biggest worry was whether the coffee machine would be fixed by noon.
Then came the conference call. Your manager, Karen, with her MBA from a school
you had never heard of, decided your entire department could be "optimised"
by outsourcing to an AI chatbot that cost twelve dollars a month.

After weeks of sending resumes into the void you finally got a call back. Not
from a tech company. From Earl. Earl was the night-shift operator at Riverside
for 37 years. He was three weeks from retirement when he decided to test whether
the plant could run itself while he took a nap. It could not. The resulting
incident involved a minor steam leak, the evacuation of two city blocks, and
Earl's immediate retirement without the gold watch he had been promised.

The plant needed a new operator. Desperately. Your resume mentioned you
"worked with computers" and "solved complex problems." Close enough.

Your training lasted exactly four hours. The plant supervisor - a grizzled man
named Chuck who smells permanently of diesel and disappointment - walked you
through the control room once.

He pointed at the pressure readings: \textit{"Keep it around 160 bar. Too low,
no power. Too high, boom."}

He pointed at the drum level gauge: \textit{"Keep water between 40\% and 60\%.
Too low, tubes overheat. Too high, turbine gets wet. Wet turbines are expensive.
Don't make the turbine wet."}

He gestured at the pulverizers: \textit{"These grind coal into dust. They
vibrate. Sometimes too much. If one trips, start another. We have four. Try to
keep at least two running."}

Then Chuck handed you a coffee-stained operations manual from 1987, said
\textit{"You'll figure it out,"} and left to deal with a situation in Unit 2.

\begin{notebox}
Read the gauges. Press the buttons. Don't panic.
\end{notebox}

\section{How a Coal Power Plant Works}

You are responsible for a 300 MW coal-fired power station. Understanding what
you are operating will help you keep it alive. This is the short version. The
1987 manual has 400 pages of the long version. Nobody reads the long version.

\subsection{Stage 1: Coal System - Fuel Preparation}

Raw coal arrives by conveyor and is fed into four \textbf{PULVERIZERS} - large
grinding mills that reduce it to a fine powder. Pulverised coal is blown into
the boiler furnace by primary air fans where it ignites and burns at
approximately 1400°C. More coal burning = more heat = more steam. You control
coal burn rate by adjusting each pulverizer's load from 0\% to 100\%.
Overloading them causes vibration and motor overheating.

\subsection{Stage 2: Boiler - Steam Generation}

The boiler is a large vessel surrounded by water tubes. Heat from the burning
coal converts that water to high-pressure steam. The target conditions are
160 bar pressure and 530°C temperature. A drum at the top of the boiler
maintains the water level - this must stay close to 50\%. Too low and the
tubes overheat and rupture. Too high and water carryover damages the turbine.
The \textbf{FEEDWATER SYSTEM} continuously replaces the water that boils off.

\subsection{Stage 3: Turbine - Mechanical Energy}

High-pressure steam from the boiler flows through the \textbf{MAIN STEAM VALVE}
into the turbine. The steam expands across multiple blade stages, spinning the
turbine shaft at 3000 RPM (50 Hz). The shaft drives the \textbf{GENERATOR}
which produces the electrical output. Opening the steam valve increases power;
closing it reduces power. The turbine responds to valve changes within 5-15
seconds.

\subsection{Stage 4: Cooling System - Heat Rejection}

Steam exhausted from the turbine enters the \textbf{CONDENSER}. Three
\textbf{CIRCULATION PUMPS} draw cool water from the river through the condenser,
removing heat and condensing the steam back into water. This creates a vacuum
(0.046 bar) in the condenser - the lower the vacuum pressure, the more
efficiently the turbine extracts energy from the steam. Condensed water is
returned to the boiler by the feedwater pumps, completing the cycle.

\subsection{Your Job}

Balance all four stages to match the power demand issued by the grid operator
(TSO) while keeping the plant within safe limits. Equipment will degrade.
Faults will occur. The TSO does not care about any of that. Your job is to keep
the lights on.

\textbf{\uppercase{Equipment Health:}}

All seven major systems (BOILER, TURBINE, COAL, FEEDWATER, COOLING, CONTROL,
ELECTRICAL) carry degraded health into the next shift. Health is tracked as
\textbf{Equivalent Operating Hours (EOH)} --- the higher the EOH, the lower
the health. Poor management and excessive start/stop cycles accelerate EOH
accumulation. Low health increases the probability and severity of faults.

\textbf{\uppercase{Maintenance System}} (spending available from Shift 5 onward):

Maintenance points are earned from Shift 1. From Shift 5 onward, you may
spend them to reduce equipment EOH before the next shift. Each maintenance
tier reduces EOH uniformly across \textit{all} components. Maintenance is
not available if the plant tripped.

\section{The Control Room}

Your control room has five information pages accessible at any time with the
function keys. A KEY INDICATORS BOX in the upper-right corner of every page
always shows the most critical plant parameters.

\textbf{\uppercase{Key Indicators Box (all pages):}}

\begin{Verbatim}
POWER: 247 MW   PRESSURE: 163 bar  TEMP: 520 oC  DRUM: 50 %  COAL: 13 kg/s
\end{Verbatim}

Five numbers. Always visible. Always honest. If any of them is heading in the
wrong direction, something needs your attention now --- regardless of which page
you are on. Target: POWER matches the TSO order; PRESSURE 155--165 bar; TEMP
520--535°C; DRUM 40--60\%; COAL appropriate for current load.

The plant STATUS banner below the box reads \texttt{NORMAL}, \texttt{WARNING},
\texttt{ALARM}, or \texttt{TRIPPED}. If it is not \texttt{NORMAL}, find out why
before doing anything else.

\subsection{\key{F1} Main Ops}

Your home page. You will run most of your shift from here without switching away.

\textbf{TSO ORDER panel} (top left) --- current target MW, your actual output,
and whether you are IN RANGE. This is the only number the TSO cares about.
Watch the countdown; if it starts dropping fast, stop fine-tuning and hit
the target.

\textbf{BOILER panel} (top centre) --- pressure, drum level, and steam
temperature at a glance. Three numbers that tell you whether the boiler is
happy. If pressure is drifting and you have not touched the coal load, suspect
a leak.

\textbf{FEEDWATER and COOLING panels} (bottom row) --- pump status and valve
position. If a pump shows STOPPED unexpectedly, act immediately. Drum level
will not wait.

\textbf{PLANT SCHEMATIC} (right column) --- a live diagram of the full plant.
Running equipment is highlighted; failed equipment goes dark. Use it to confirm
your mental model when multiple things are happening at once.

\subsection{\key{F2} Coal System} \textit{(Unlocked at Shift 4.)}

Four pulverizers, one table. Switch here the moment vibration or temperature
misbehaves on the main page.

\textbf{Watch:} VIBRATION column. Green is fine; approaching 60 means reduce
load; 80 is an alarm; 95 trips the mill automatically. MOTOR TEMP: alarm at
85°C, trip at 92°C. Catch either early and shed load before the mill forces
your hand.

\textbf{Summary line} at the bottom shows total coal output (kg/s). Cross-check
this against the COAL figure in the Key Indicators Box --- if they disagree, a
feeder jam may have partially cut output.

\subsection{\key{F3} Cooling System} \textit{(Unlocked at Shift 5.)}

Cooling is invisible until it goes wrong, then it quietly destroys your
efficiency rating.

\textbf{Condenser panel} (left) --- vacuum and fouling percentage are the two
numbers to watch. Fouling above 30\% means run a backwash \key{B}. A degraded
vacuum costs you turbine efficiency; at full degradation you lose around 7\%
output for the same fuel burn.

\textbf{Circ Pump table} (right) --- three pumps, bearing temperature, and
vibration. Normally A and B run; C is standby. If a pump trips, start C
immediately --- do not wait for the tripped pump's 20-second cooldown.

\subsection{\key{F4} Water/Steam Cycle} \textit{(Unlocked at Shift 3.)}

The detailed view of everything between the drum and the turbine. Go here when
the drum level is misbehaving and \key{F1} does not give you enough information.

\textbf{Feedwater Pump table} (top) --- status, load, bearing temperature,
and vibration for both pumps. Pump A shows no power reading because it is
turbine-driven. If bearing temperature on either pump is climbing toward 80°C,
back off its load or prepare the other pump.

\textbf{Boiler Heat Balance} (bottom) --- coal feed in versus steam out. Any
active tube leak appears here as unexplained steam loss. If the numbers do not
add up, you have a leak.

\subsection{\key{F5} Equipment Health}

Check this at the start of each shift and after any fault. It tells you how
much trouble is waiting for you.

\textbf{Systems Health} (left) --- bar graphs for Boiler, Coal, Feedwater,
Cooling, and Turbine. A system below 50\% health will start generating faults
with increasing frequency. Plan your load accordingly.

\textbf{EOH table} (centre) --- Equivalent Operating Hours per component.
Higher EOH means higher fault probability. This is what maintenance spending
reduces between shifts.

\textbf{Shift Statistics} (right) --- TSO order results, alarm and fault counts,
fuel efficiency, and current auxiliary load. Your end-of-shift grade is
calculated from these numbers. Check efficiency mid-shift if you are running
heavy on coal.

\subsection{Alarms \& Messages Panel (all pages)}

Six-line event log at the bottom of every page.
GREEN = information; YELLOW = TSO order expired; RED = alarm, fault, or trip.

Alarms freeze your attention until you acknowledge them with \key{SPACE}. Do
not acknowledge blindly --- read the message first, identify the page you need
(\key{F2} for coal, \key{F3} for cooling, \key{F4} for feedwater and boiler),
then act. Acknowledging an alarm does not fix the underlying problem.

\section{Plant Systems}

\subsection{Main Steam Valve}

The primary power control. Opening the valve allows more steam to reach the
turbine; closing it reduces flow. Power responds within 5-15 seconds. This is
the first control you learn and the one you will use most often.

Select: \key{S} \quad Adjust: \key{UP} / \key{DOWN} ($\pm$0.5\% per step) \quad
Range: 0\% to 100\% \quad Effect: $\pm$0.5-2 MW per 1\% valve change.

\begin{notebox}
Make small, deliberate adjustments. A 10\% lurch on the steam valve will upset
the boiler pressure and drum level simultaneously. Chuck calls this "the domino
effect." It is not complimentary.
\end{notebox}

\subsection{Feedwater System}

Water must be continuously supplied to replace what boils off. Two pumps provide
redundancy: \key{Q} Pump A is turbine-driven (turbo-pump --- it runs from HP steam
extraction and draws no electrical power). \key{A} Pump B is motor-driven and
consumes 4.0 MW of auxiliary power. Prefer Pump A in normal operation; use Pump B
as backup or when A is unavailable. Nominal flow: 265 kg/s at 100\% valve.

Both pumps are monitored for bearing temperature (alarm 80°C, trip 88°C) and
vibration (warn 55\%, alarm 75\%, auto-trip 90\%). Monitor the \key{F4} pump table for
early warning signs. After a pump trips, a 10-second cooldown is required before
restart.

\begin{notebox}
If the drum level drops below 40\%, open the feedwater valve. If it rises above
60\%, close it. If both pumps fail simultaneously, pray. Then reduce load
immediately and start the backup.
\end{notebox}

\subsection{Coal Pulverizers (A, B, C, D)}

Four mills grind coal to powder and deliver it to the furnace. Total coal burn
rate determines the boiler's heat input and therefore the maximum achievable
steam output and power.

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{lcc}
\toprule
\textbf{Parameter} & \textbf{Warning} & \textbf{Trip} \\
\midrule
Mill Load     & $>$90\%  & N/A     \\
Vibration     & $>$60    & $>$95   \\
Motor Temp.   & $>$85°C  & $>$92°C \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Motor power draw: up to 0.5 MW per pulverizer at 100\% load (scales with load \%). Running
all four at full load consumes $\sim$2 MW of auxiliary load.

\subsection{Cooling System}

Three circulation pumps move river water through the condenser to remove heat
from the exhaust steam. Adequate cooling maintains the condenser vacuum - poor
vacuum reduces turbine efficiency significantly (−7\% at fully degraded vacuum).

Pumps A \key{Z} and B \key{X} normally running. Pump C \key{C} standby.
Backwash \key{B} clears river debris (90 sec). Target vacuum: 0.046 bar.
Backwash recommended when fouling exceeds 25--30\%. Each pump draws 2.5 MW of
auxiliary electrical power when running. After stopping, a 20-second cooldown
must elapse before a pump can restart.

\subsection{Turbine / Generator}

The turbine converts steam energy into shaft rotation at 3000 RPM, driving
the generator. You do not directly control the turbine beyond the main steam
valve. Nominal output: 300 MW. Efficiency improves with higher condenser vacuum.

\section{TSO Orders - Meeting Demand}

The Transmission System Operator (TSO) manages the national grid and issues
power output targets to all generators. Your job is to match those targets
within the compliance window before the order expires.

An order is COMPLETE when you maintain power within the tolerance band
continuously for the required hold duration.

\section{Operator's Guide - Efficient Operation}

\subsection{Power Ramping Strategy}

When a new TSO order arrives, resist the urge to slam the steam valve to the
new target. The correct sequence is:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Anticipate the load direction.} Is the new target higher or lower?
  \item \textbf{Adjust the steam valve first.} This gives immediate power response.
  \item \textbf{Adjust coal feed to match.} More power = more coal. Allow 10-15 seconds
        for coal burn changes to affect steam pressure.
  \item \textbf{Balance feedwater.} Higher firing rate means more steam; open the
        feedwater valve slightly to maintain drum level.
  \item \textbf{Wait and observe.} Let the plant stabilise before further adjustment.
        Power oscillations usually damp out in 15-30 seconds.
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Rule of thumb:} Move the valve, wait, then move coal. Never move both
at maximum rate simultaneously.

\subsection{Pulverizer Load Balancing}

Running all four mills at 50\% is safer than running two at 100\%. Spread the
load evenly across running mills to minimise vibration, reduce motor
temperatures, and extend equipment life.

% Recommended loading: 2 mills $\to$ 55-65\% each; 3 mills $\to$ 45-55\% each;
% 4 mills $\to$ 40-50\% each. Keep all running mills below 90\%.

Watch vibration closely. A mill climbing toward 60 units should have its load
reduced. A mill at 80 is alarming. At 95 it will trip automatically.

\subsection{Feedwater Pump Strategy}

Normally only one pump needs to run. Run both when output is above 270 MW,
one pump shows degradation warnings, or you anticipate a rapid load increase.
If a pump trips unexpectedly, start the other immediately --- you have
approximately 10 seconds before drum level drops to alarm. Pump A \key{Q}
is turbine-driven (no electrical load --- always prefer it as primary). Pump B
\key{A} is motor-driven and consumes 4.0 MW. Keep Pump A running whenever possible;
switch to Pump B only when A has tripped or is in cooldown.

\subsection{Cooling Pump Strategy}

Two circulation pumps (A and B) are normally running. Pump C is the backup.
Never run fewer than two pumps at full load --- insufficient cooling raises
condenser back-pressure and reduces turbine efficiency by up to 7\%. Check fouling
percentage on the \key{F3} page periodically; backwash when fouling exceeds
25--30\%. Monitor bearing temperature (alarm at 80°C) and vibration on the pump
table. If a pump trips, remember the 20-second cooldown before it can restart ---
start Pump C immediately rather than waiting.

\subsection{Responding to Faults}

When an alarm appears: (1) note the fault type; (2) check the relevant page
(\key{F2} coal, \key{F3} cooling, \key{F4} feedwater/boiler); (3) take the
prescribed response from Section 10; (4) acknowledge the alarm \key{SPACE};
(5) monitor the outcome.

Most time-critical faults: Feedwater pump trip (10 sec to start backup);
circ pump trip (30 sec to restore cooling); major tube leak (immediate load
reduction).

\subsection{Reading Trends, Not Snapshots}

A drum level of 42\% is not alarming. A drum level falling from 55\% to 42\%
over the last 20 seconds is alarming. Always assess whether a value is stable,
rising, or falling. A parameter near a warning threshold but stable is usually
fine. A parameter heading toward the limit at speed requires intervention
\textit{now}, not when the alarm activates.

\subsection{Efficiency Tips}

Efficiency score is based on coal burned relative to electricity produced.

Avoid unnecessary load cycling --- combustion efficiency drops temporarily
during rapid ramps. Match coal feed to actual demand: do not run four mills
at 70\% when two at 75\% would produce the same output. Maintain good
condenser vacuum. Avoid high drum level, as water carryover reduces steam
quality and turbine efficiency.

\textbf{Auxiliary load:} pumps and mills draw from your own output before the
meter reaches the grid. The \key{F5} Shift Statistics box shows the live total.

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{llr}
\toprule
\textbf{Equipment} & \textbf{Notes} & \textbf{Load (MW)} \\
\midrule
Feedwater Pump A  & Steam-driven                         & --- \\
Feedwater Pump B  & Motor-driven                           & 4.0 \\
Circ Pump A       & Per pump when running                  & 2.5 \\
Circ Pump B       & Per pump when running                  & 2.5 \\
Circ Pump C       & Per pump when running                  & 2.5 \\
Pulverizer (each) & At 100\% load; scales with load \%     & 0.5 \\
\midrule
\textit{Worst case} & \textit{FW-B + 3 circ + 4 pulv @ 100\%} & \textit{13.5} \\
\textit{Best case}  & \textit{FW-A + 2 circ + 2 pulv @ 50\%}  & \textit{5.5}  \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Prefer Pump A whenever possible. Run only the circ pumps you need. Keep
pulverizer loads balanced and no higher than demand requires. The difference
between worst and best case is nearly 8 MW --- enough to push a borderline
TSO order into compliance.


\begin{notebox}
The operators who score A-grades are not faster than the others. They make
fewer large corrections. Small movements, steady hands, read the trend. That is
the whole secret. You are welcome.
\end{notebox}
